Predicting Substation Overload from Home EV Charging

A simulation analyzing the thermal load on Auckland's 100 kVA neighborhood street transformers during peak 6:00 PM – 10:00 PM charging windows.

01 / SUBSTATION

The Green Street Box

A typical residential street runs off a single 100 kVA ground-mount transformer. Built decades ago, it was sized for standard home lighting and appliances.

02 / LOAD SPIKE

The 7.4 kW EV Draw

A Level-2 home EV charger draws 32 Amps continuously (7.4 kW). Just 10 to 12 EVs charging simultaneously can fully saturate an entire street's capacity.

03 / CONGESTION

The 6:00 PM Peak

When residents return home and plug in concurrently at dinner, high domestic demand plus vehicle charging risks overheating transformer windings and blowing main fuses.

Technical Specifications

Select any category to expand physical equations and circuit diagrams.

Single-phase real power is governed by P = V × I × cos(θ). With standard Level-2 chargers running at unity power factor on a 230 V supply at 32 A, the demand is precisely 7.36 kW.

Uncoordinated single-phase vehicle allocation across 3-phase street mains induces neutral return current and voltage unbalance between phases.

Substation transformers do not trip immediately at 101% load. Large thermal mass in the mineral insulating oil buffers short peaks with a 2 to 3 hour time constant.

However, when EV charging lasts continuously for 5+ hours, core temperatures exceed 110°C, accelerating insulation paper degradation exponentially.

Rather than assuming every car plugs in simultaneously, we simulate real driver patterns with probability distributions: commuting distance (battery depth-of-discharge) and return-home arrival times.

This allows grid planners to determine the exact EV penetration threshold (e.g., 28% of homes) before street fuses trip.
